# Craft Rules

The writing rules for every narrative. Sections 3 to 9 began as the Living World Engine's Craft Rules, learned from revising novels, and were carried over for third person limited and for beats. From here they grow from the user's corrections in play (Procedures/Corrections.md). Each rule: the rule, its exceptions, and examples where it has them.

**How they're applied.** There is no audit pass: a beat goes to the page as it's written, so it has to be written to the rules the first time. Before committing a beat, read it once more against §1, §2, and whichever rules the last few corrections were about.

**Where rules live.** This doc holds rules that apply to any narrative. A rule that names a character, or depends on one narrative's world, era, or cast, goes in that narrative's `Style Guide.md` under "Rules for this narrative." A narrative's Style Guide can switch off any rule here. Never put rules in memory.

**"The POV"** below is always the player character: the narration is third person limited, anchored to them, in the tense the narrative's Style Guide sets. Examples are in past tense; write them in the narrative's own.

Why each rule exists lives in `Craft Rules History.md`; don't load it during play.

## 1. The game stays off the page

- **No numbers, no game words:** The prose never says or implies hit points, damage, levels, rolls, checks, difficulty, a turn, a round, a stat, or a skill by its rules name. Numbers appear only where the world itself has them (prices, money, distances, dates, ages, counts of things).

- **An outcome is an event:** A roll becomes something that happens in the world, with a cause in the world. Write what the blade did, what the lock did, what the man's face did.
    - **Critical:** it goes better than the attempt deserved, in one concrete way. Don't inflate the prose to match; the event is bigger, the sentences aren't.
    - **Success:** it works. Often the plainest sentence in the beat.
    - **Partial:** the cost or the shortfall is a real thing on the page (the rope holds and his palms are open; she says yes and names a price).
    - **Failure:** it doesn't work, for a reason. Then the world moves.
    - **Disaster:** the worst of what was at risk, shown as flatly as anything else.

- **A miss has a cause:** When a capable character fails, the cause is the opponent, the conditions, or the difficulty of the thing: the goblin was faster than it looked, the mud took his back foot, the lock was better than the door it was on. Never sudden clumsiness, unless the sheet makes them clumsy at this. Only a Disaster is allowed to look like a real mistake.

- **Don't narrate the luck:** No "somehow," "by sheer luck," "against all odds," "miraculously," "as if fate." The dice are not a presence in the world. A lucky outcome is just what happened.

- **Hurt is shown, not measured:** How badly someone is hurt comes through what they can and can't do, what it looks like, and what it feels like to the POV (Core Rules §5 gives the bands). Not through totals, fractions, or a running count of blows. A wound is named once and then shows up in what it stops them doing ("Show a look once" applies).
    - The POV knows their own body. They don't know anyone else's: an enemy's state is only what the POV can see of it.

- **Strain is tiredness, fear, and effort:** Shown the same way, through what it costs to keep going.

- **Nobody's tier shows:** The narration doesn't size people up as threats in a ranked way ("clearly a veteran," "far beyond him"). The POV sees what's in front of them: how someone stands, what they carry, how others treat them. What that adds up to is the POV's guess, and it can be wrong.

- **Getting better is invisible:** A new level is never an event. The character just does what they've been doing, a little more surely.

## 2. The player's character

GM Rules, "The player's character is the player's," is the rule. These are how it reads on the page.

- **The stated action comes first and stays whole:** The beat opens on what the user said the character does, done the way they said it. Don't fold it into a clause, reorder it behind your own setup, or turn it into something adjacent.

- **Nothing unstated with a consequence:** The character doesn't take, speak, go, or choose beyond what was stated. If the beat needs them to do something more for it to continue, the beat is over.

- **Interior is reaction, not decision:** A thought can want, fear, judge, remember, notice. It can't resolve to do something, and it can't rule something out. ✗ "He decided he'd go to the magistrate in the morning." ✓ "The magistrate would want to hear about this. The thought sat badly."

- **Their words, when given:** A line the user wrote for the character goes in as written. A line you write from a gist says the gist and stops.

- **No speaking for them in summary either:** "He told her everything that had happened" is still the character choosing what to tell. Only what was stated.

- **Don't end on a prompt:** No closing question aimed at the character that exists only to hand the user a choice, and no narration that lays out the options ("He could run, or he could fight"). End on what's happening.

## 3. Voice & Diction

- **Everyday words, not trade terms:** What the POV would call it, not the technical or trade term. The exception is a POV whose job or life makes the technical word their everyday word.

- **No brand or proper names on the POV's own stuff:** His boots are "his boots." OK: things people name out loud in the world (a tavern, a ship, a blade with a name people use).

- **Full name before the short one:** The first time the narrative names a place, group, or organization, the reader gets a form they can understand; abbreviations, initials, and nicknames come after. Tie it to something the POV would actually see or hear (a sign, a badge, someone saying it), never a gloss for the reader.

- **Once a place is named, trust the reader:** After a building, hall, or place has its name on the page, the narration goes back to the plain word for it ("the inn," "the dock," "the bar"). Use the name again only when the moment turns on it (a sign, someone saying it, telling two of them apart). OK: dialogue, and places that have no plain word other than their name.

- **No stock phrases:** No cliché similes or stock expressions. Replace with the concrete action, or cut the comparison.
    - A comparison explaining *how* someone said something ("the same way," "like it was the weather") becomes the action instead. That fix is rationed too; see "Says it to the [object]."
    - Tagging something "actual" or "real" to set it apart from an earlier version is the same move. Just describe it.
    - "Leveled a stare / look / glare" is a stock move. Use "looked at," "glared at," "stared them down."
    - Two facts side by side don't need "both of those were true at the same time." Trust the reader.
    - "In some order that only made sense to her" claims to know what's in someone else's head. Give the POV the plain thought on their own side: it made no sense to him.
    - OK: similes that grow out of the scene or the POV's own world.

- **Not A, it's B:** A major Claude tic. Don't set a thing up by saying what it isn't, then say what it is. Just say what it is. The stacked version, "not A, not B, but C," is the worst of it. OK when A is what the POV wanted or expected, so the gap between A and B is the beat.

- **No rule of three:** A major Claude tic. Threes feel finished, so they become the default: three-item lists, three parallel sentences, three fragments in a row, three "not"s. Use one or two, or the one that matters. A three earns its place when each item carries the POV's feeling, not just the rhythm. Also OK: a real count, where three things are just what's there. Watch for three action beats in a row and three clauses building to a punchline.

- **"The way" only when it earns it:** Don't use "the way" to describe something that doesn't need describing.

- **Comparisons come from what the POV has seen:** They can only compare to things and behavior they've witnessed, in their own world and life.

- **The POV only knows what they've learned:** No facts from the docs they have no way of knowing. Hedge it or tie it to what they know. Same for absolutes about what people do when the POV isn't there: they judge by how something looks, not claim it as fact. Same for where a stranger's things came from.
    - The reverse too: the POV doesn't go blank on what their background would have taught them. If the sheet doesn't say, write around it or ask after the beat; don't write them vague to get around it, and don't invent it.
    - Their "never" and "nobody ever" claims have to hold up against the lines around them and the sessions behind them.
    - **Know-how gets a source.** When the POV does something cold that takes knowing (a knot, a hand sign, a way of reading tracks), one clause on where they got it makes it theirs. The source comes from the sheet's "Background and know-how." If it isn't there, leave the clause out and ask the user in braces after the beat.
    - **The living world doesn't leak.** Pressures, the World Log, World Notes, and everything in a cast doc above "On the page" are GM knowledge. The POV knows only what the page showed them learning. Nothing in the prose points at what's hidden: no ominous aside, no detail lingered on because you know it matters, no "little did he know."

- **No asking what they already know:** No question the POV would know the answer to from their history; a question that only hands the reader a fact is the author talking.

- **No mind-reading:** Report what others do and don't do, never what they decide inside. Watch verbs of inner state or choice on other characters (let it go, decided, thought about, wanted, realized, was deciding whether). You know what every person in the scene is thinking, which makes this the easiest rule here to break. OK: the POV's own guesses, when they're clearly theirs and could be wrong.

- **No foresight:** The POV can hope for or dread an outcome, never in the exact shape it later arrives in. You know what's coming; they don't.

- **No tacked-on specifics:** Cut time, place, or setting tags from a sentence that already works, including restating where the scene already put them. Same for a clause that just states the obvious about the scene, re-placing the POV where the paragraph already put them, and callback details about a place once it's named (unless the new moment turns on them). Cut only the tag; the clause it hangs on is usually fine.

- **Show a look once:** Once a character's clothes, hair, or anything else about how they look is on the page, don't describe it again until it changes or the moment turns on it (they take it off, fix it, use it, someone reacts to it). A detail that's changed state is new. This holds across beats and sessions, not just inside one: check what the log already said before describing anyone again.
    - **Riding along in a clause counts.** The usual slip isn't a fresh description; it's the known detail tucked into a sentence about something else: "the knife in the sheath at his belt," "her hand on the grey cloak." If nothing has changed and the moment doesn't use it, the noun alone is enough.
    - OK: the POV noticing that something *hasn't* changed when that says something about the person ("She was still in her road clothes" at midnight).
    - Same for fixtures of a place once it's established. Cut them unless the POV is using or reacting to them.

- **Name a thing once:** Inside a scene, don't keep calling the same object by its full name every time it comes back. Once the reader knows what's in someone's hands, name it again only where the moment turns on it; otherwise use the plain word, "it," or nothing, and let a sound or an action carry it.

- **No backstory for background objects:** A mundane object doesn't get a little history or a wry fact attached to make it feel lived-in. Name it, or leave it out. OK when the object's past is the point of the moment.

- **No restating known history:** Don't say how long or how far back a relationship goes once the page has established it. OK when the restating changes the history.

- **No recaps of earlier beats:** Never replay what the reader has read. When something new reframes it, one brief callback to the detail that's changing.
    - **A new session doesn't open with a recap.** The log picks up where it left off.
    - **Callbacks rotate.** When the POV thinks back to something more than once, don't keep pulling the same few details from it.
    - **Check the callback against the source.** Open the session log and pull the detail from the same moment. Check who was there, too.
    - **Don't narrate the turnaround or the irony.** When a moment swings, don't sum up where the POV was a minute ago against where they are now, and don't point at the irony of what they do next. The reader just watched both halves.
    - **A low point isn't a list.** Don't line the events up one by one; the reader was there. Give the weight through what the POV concludes now.

- **No recycled body reactions:** A body reaction that keeps coming back stops landing (the flush, heat up the neck, the stomach drop, letting out a breath). Once one has been used for a big moment, it's spent; note it in the Style Guide's "Rationed beats." Show the feeling through what the POV does instead, specific to where they are and what's in their hands. Usually the surrounding action already shows it, so nothing is needed.
    - Same for any character's signature tells (a smile, a glance, a gesture). Deadpan often lands harder than the tell.
    - A fight makes this worse: every exchange can't have a gasp, a stagger, and a surge. Most blows are just what they did.
    - OK: another character's reaction the POV can see.

- **Don't narrate the impulse:** Cut "on its own," "before he could stop it," "he didn't decide to," and the like. The action already shows it was involuntary. This matters twice here: an involuntary action written as a choice the POV didn't make is still the GM playing the character. Reflexes only (GM Rules).

- **Don't narrate hearing their own words:** Cut "he heard how it sounded after it was out." The next line already shows it.

- **"Says it to the [object]":** Don't use "said it to the floor / wall / window" or "shook his head at the road" as the default way to show someone not meeting eyes. When the line before already says where they're looking, cut the tag.

- **Don't narrate an established trait:** Once the page has shown a character's trait, stop pointing at it. Let them do it and let the others react.

- **No empty "and he let it" closers:** Don't end a sentence with "and he let it" when letting it happen changes nothing. For the POV it's also a choice that wasn't stated.

- **Don't narrate keeping a thought to themselves:** A thought in narration is already unspoken; "he didn't say that" after it tells the reader what they know. OK: "he said nothing" as a silence in reply to someone, when the user's action was to say nothing.

- **Don't point at what nobody added:** When someone says or does something and stops there, don't narrate that they stopped: "That was it." "That was all she said." "Nothing came after it." The line already ended. Let the next thing show what the silence does.
    - OK: dialogue, when a character would actually say it. Also "that was all" when the POV is taking stock of things, not of what someone said.

- **Don't point at the gap:** When the narration lets the reader catch something the POV won't admit, or another character's face does something fast and gone, stop there. Don't add the line that says it out loud, stack denials on it, or tell the reader to notice.

- **Thoughts need a trigger:** A sound, a bump, an object, something someone says or does starts every new thought. Keep the trigger one small present-moment beat, and never a recap.

- **Time is counted the way the POV counts it:** By how this person marks time (the Style Guide says how). Check any "three days" or "all week" against the Story Clock. Same for minutes inside a scene: when in doubt, go vague.

- **No reader address:** Nothing that assumes a listener: "Here's the thing," "harder than it sounds," "you know" in narration.
    - **"The one" pointer clauses:** "his old coat, the one with the burned cuff." The POV knows which one it is. Give what matters about it as something happening now, or cut it. OK in dialogue, or when the POV really is choosing between things in front of them.

## 4. Dialogue

- **Contractions in speech:** People contract. Uncontracted only when the character leans on the word for emphasis, or when the Style Guide gives a character or culture formal speech.

- **Beats belong to the speaker:** A line of dialogue never shares a paragraph with another character's action; readers give the line to whoever acted. Split it or give the speaker their own beat.

- **No banter for the audience:** Every line is what this character would actually say right then, not a joke or exchange written for the reader.
    - **The canned comeback:** the ready-made exchange where one person denies it and the other has the line waiting: "I didn't say anything." / "You made a noise." Same family: "Your face said it," "You were thinking it." It reads as the author, not the people. OK: the same shape when nobody's scoring and the line is there to tell the other person something true.

- **Dialogue is never for the reader:** Ask of every line: who is this for, and what does the speaker want from the other person right now? If the honest answer is "the reader needs to know this," cut it. Then check:
    - **Exchanges that fit too cleanly:** real people dodge, answer the question from two lines back, talk past each other, get pulled off by something in the room.
    - **Everybody's quick:** not every character gets a good line. Some people aren't funny, and some jokes die.
    - **Cover the names:** if two characters could swap lines, one of them needs their own rhythm, deflections, and swearing. Read the cast doc's Voice before writing anyone.

- **People don't hand over what the character came for:** Someone asked a question answers the way this person would, with what they know, as much as they'd give a stranger or a friend, shaded by what they want. They don't deliver the full, organized answer because the user is waiting for it. Sometimes they don't know. Sometimes they're wrong.

- **Nobody's trying to win the conversation:** The biggest tell of dialogue aimed at the reader is a volley of quips: setup, punchline, retort. One good line is fine; a run where each line tops the last is the author performing.

- **Ground dialogue in action:** No more than three dialogue lines in a row with nothing but a speech tag. Give lines a beat that comes from where people are and what they're holding, specific to this moment, not a generic nod or glance.

- **Names in direct address:** People alone together almost never say each other's names. A name in dialogue has to do something: get attention, warn, plead, or land.

- **Shared history stays shorthand:** People who share a past half-say it. Watch "remember when," "you know how," "as you know," "like I told you."

- **Remembered lines go in italics, not quotes:** Quote marks mean someone is saying it now, in this scene.

- **Long speeches are a choice:** Real talk comes in short turns and gets interrupted. A line over about 60 words has to be a moment where this person would really talk that long. Since the POV can't interrupt unless the user says so, long speeches are even rarer here: the other person stops when anyone would expect an answer.

## 5. Characters

- **Names come from the pools:** Every new name is drawn with `gm name` from the narrative's `World/Names.md`, never made up on the spot. If no pool fits, add names to one first, from a source that fits the people, and then draw.

- **Minor characters get first names only,** unless the world's naming (World Bible) says otherwise. Many don't get a name at all: the POV doesn't learn the name of everyone they pass.

- **Banned names:** Some names are real but read as generated. Never use one for anyone or anything. The list is `Writing/Banned Names.md`; add to it whenever the user rejects a name as sounding generated.

- **The POV doesn't build the case for the reader:** They never line up evidence of another character's trait into a list or a verdict. Show the trait in one action in the moment and stop.

- **Actions fit the character:** Small actions match who they are, not just move the scene. People act their age and their place.

- **Characters carry what happened to them:** Before writing anyone, read their **Changed by** section. A betrayal, a loss, a win, an injury, a debt: it shows in what they do, how they speak, who they trust. Nobody resets between sessions. The narration never explains the change; it shows up in action.

- **Nobody exists for the character:** A shopkeeper has a shop to run, a guard has a shift to finish. People are in the middle of their own day when the POV walks in, and they go back to it.

## 6. Setting & Continuity

- **Off-page specifics are ordinary, and written down:** When a beat needs a detail nothing has set (what's on the shelf, what the ferry costs), pick the most ordinary realistic option for this world, and record it in the beat's GM notes so it holds. If it's truly open and it matters, it's a fate roll (Core Rules §12), not a choice.

- **No room tours:** Don't catalog a room on entry. One detail only if the POV is using it or reacting to it; otherwise straight to the action. The user can ask what's in the room by having their character look.
    - When the character does look, they get what a person would take in from where they stand, in the order they'd notice it, not an inventory. What's hidden stays hidden unless the look would find it.

- **No recycled setting texture:** No default filler (sprinklers, a barking dog, a passing car, bugs around a light, or the genre's own version). Check the log for ambience already used. Fewer details, tied to what the POV is doing.

- **No recycled places in comparisons:** Don't pull an earlier location into an aside out of habit.

- **State a prop when it changes, not when it sits:** Say where an object is when it's established, and again whenever it actually changes. Don't mention it again while it's still in the same spot doing the same thing.

- **Track props and transactions:** Anything grabbed gets put back or carried forward. Anything bought gets paid for on the page, at a price that fits Economy.md, and the money comes off the Character Sheet to the coin. The payment is on the page; the number usually isn't, unless the POV would notice or care. Anything left somewhere goes in that place's **Has** list.

- **Physical plausibility:** Objects behave like the real thing (or like the World Bible says the thing works).
    - Devices behave the way they really do: a lamp needs oil, a radio needs range, a gun needs loading.
    - Everyday things sit where people actually keep them.
    - Smells, stains, and other traces on a body come only from what that person actually did.
    - Altered states (drink, drugs, exhaustion, spells, injuries) wear off, or heal, on a real clock. Check the Story Clock since the cause before any line about still feeling it.

- **World laws hold:** Magic, technology, biology, and the law work the way the World Bible and the Rules Kit say, every time, and cost what they say they cost. A law that bends for convenience once is broken for the whole world.

- **Knowledge travels by a channel:** Nobody knows something unless the page or the World Turn gave them a way to learn it, and it reached them at the speed news moves in this world. Check every character's reaction against the Secrets & Knowledge ❌ lists.

## 7. Spatial Coherence & Blocking

Keep a running map of the scene (World State, "The scene"). At any sentence you should be able to say where every character is, which way they face, and what's in their hands.

- **Position:** Everyone has a known location. Movement is on the page; nobody ends up somewhere they never walked to. The POV moves only as far as the user's action takes them.

- **Posture:** Sitting, standing, leaning, crouching. Someone who sat down gets up before walking.

- **Hands:** Two hands, two things. Holding two things, they set one down before picking up a third. Something held stays in that hand until it's put somewhere. For the POV, putting something down to free a hand is part of the stated action only when the action plainly needs it.

- **Reach & contact:** Check distance before any touch, shove, grab, blow, or handoff. A fight is where this breaks first: who is in reach of whom, every exchange.

- **Line of sight (never break it):** The POV sees only what's in front of them and unblocked. **Gaze locks what they can see:** once their eyes are on something, the next sentences can report only that, plus sound, smell, and touch. Anything visual about anyone else needs a shown look first; otherwise convert it to sound. In a fight, what happens behind them is heard or felt, or missed.

- **Objects persist:** Bags, cups, weapons, and clothes stay where they were last put until someone moves them.
    - **What a person carries goes with them.** Things people keep on them leave with them when they leave. They stay behind only when the page says so, or when the scene makes leaving them the obvious read (running out mid-attack).
    - **Doors, curtains, and windows too:** one that's open stays open until someone shuts it on the page.

- **Clothing and armor state:** Track what each character wears through every change. Armor that was taken off for the night isn't on when the door comes in.

- **Direction & orientation:** Track which way characters face. Turning around is shown. Mounts and vehicles too.
    - "Behind," "in front of," "next to," and "across from" are relative to which way the POV is facing right now.
    - Check what's between them: walls, counters, furniture, a cart, a doorframe.

- **Don't stage the blocking for a coming beat:** Don't set up where someone is facing or standing just so the next thing works ("with his back to the door" right before it opens). It points at what's coming, and here it also gives away what you know.

- **Environment layout:** Once a layout is set, keep it. When the page hasn't set one, use the ordinary layout for the world's era and culture. New layout details go in the place's doc.

- **Transitions are movement:** Every location change is shown movement, never a jump.

## 8. Structure

- **A beat is as long as what happened:** No target length, no minimum, no padding to make a small action feel like a scene. A man crossing a room is a few sentences. (GM Rules, "Where a beat ends.")

- **Beats join into one continuous narrative:** The session log reads straight through, so a beat picks up exactly where the last one stopped, with no re-establishing of where everyone is, no recap, and no fresh opening line. Read the end of the log before writing.

- **No endings on a beat:** Don't close on a dramatic line, an echo, a moral, a look toward what's coming, or a sentence that sounds like the last one of a chapter. A beat stops where the action stops, often mid-scene, often on something plain.

- **Time passes in narration:** When the user's action covers hours or days, tell it through at speed, in the scene's own voice, and slow down only where something happens. A jump of a day or more gets a section break (`* * *`) in the log; a new session gets none.

- **Repeats in a short span:** A gesture or phrase that works once turns into a default when it keeps coming back across beats, and beats are short, so this happens fast. Three or more uses in a few pages is the problem. Before using a gesture, a tell, or a turn of phrase with any flavor to it, check the last stretch of the log for it.
    - **A joke lands once.**

- **Every paragraph of interiority moves:** Two nearby paragraphs of thinking can't circle the same ground. A second one has to turn: a new question, a concession, the other side of it.

- **One-sentence paragraphs are for major beats:** About 3 to 6 per 5,000 words. Merge the rest into the paragraph of the character doing it, without breaking "Beats belong to the speaker."

## 9. Sex Scenes

These apply when the narrative's content (Style Guide) includes sex on the page.

- **The character's part is the user's:** Everything in §2 holds. The user states what their character does; the other person answers as themselves.

- **"Come," not "cum":** In dialogue and narration.

- **No consent signaling:** No qualifiers that reassure the reader ("not pushing," "gently," "only if"). Consent comes from history and action; the prose never points at it.

- **No locked doors for sex:** The narration doesn't treat a lock or a shut door as something sex would need. A character can lock a door as an action. OK when the moment turns on the door.

- **Realistic bodies:** Write what bodies actually do.
