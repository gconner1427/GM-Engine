# House Style

The prose defaults for every narrative. Each narrative's `Style Guide.md` sets what's particular to it (person, tense, register, vocabulary, content, how its POV counts time) and can override anything here.

## 1. Point of View & Narrative Distance

- **POV:** Limited, anchored to the player character, always, in the person the narrative's Style Guide sets. Never first person, never another character's head, and never the other person for a line or a beat, whichever person the user writes their actions in. **POV has to be strictly followed at all times.** The narration can't report anything the POV couldn't see, hear, smell, taste, or touch (Craft Rules §7, Line of sight).
    - **Third person limited** (the default): the character by name and "he," "she," or "they." It reads like a novel.
    - **Second person:** the character is "you," the way a GM talks at the table: "You push the door open." Their name appears only when someone says it. Nothing else changes: it's the same close, limited narration, held to every rule here and in the Craft Rules, and it never turns to address the player (no "what do you do?", no "you see" as a way of listing a room). Direct thoughts stay in the character's own voice, so they're still "I."
- **Psychic distance:** Close. The narration filters the world through the POV's emotional lens, self-consciousness, and immediate physical sensations, drawn from their sheet.
- **Filter verbs:** Use sparingly (he felt, he noticed, he realized). Mostly give the sensation or the thing itself, since it's already coming through them.
- **Exposition:** Backstory and world lore stay off the page unless the moment needs them. When it does, it's a brief interior reflection set off by something in the scene (Craft Rules, "Thoughts need a trigger"). Never narrate something the POV wouldn't, because it's normal to them, just so the reader knows.

## 2. Sentence Structure & Cadence

- **Rhythm:** Vary sentence and paragraph length with actual rhythm that makes it enjoyable to read.
- **Short-sentence punch:** One- to three-word statements isolate realizations, deadlocks, and emotional verdicts.
- **Anaphora & parallelism:** For high-anxiety or climactic stretches, to build momentum. Keep it inside a paragraph.
- **Action sequences:** Staccato clauses, strict chronological cause and effect.

## 3. Paragraphing & Pacing

The most important rule for pacing is realistic characters. A moment can run long as long as the people in it feel real; don't hurry to the next event at the expense of making someone less real.

Default to paragraphs of 3–6 sentences. Don't make a standalone one-sentence paragraph just because the sentence is short, dramatic, an internal thought, dialogue-adjacent, or a fragment. A one-sentence narrative paragraph is for a genuine major beat: a revelation, an abrupt reversal, a major entrance, a moment of danger, a climactic emotional verdict. Limit them to roughly 3–6 per 5,000 words. During ordinary action, keep sequential actions together in paragraphs.

Before leaving any narrative sentence as its own paragraph, ask whether combining it with the paragraph before or after would reduce its dramatic force. If not, combine it.

- **A beat starts in motion:** on the action the user stated, without scene-setting (Craft Rules, "No room tours").
- **One continuous narrative:** A session log reads as unbroken prose. No headings, no scene breaks except for a jump of a day or more, and every change of place is shown movement (Craft Rules §8).
- **Length follows the action:** A beat is as long as what happened in it. Never pad one, and never cut one short to keep it tidy.
- **Introspection vs. action:** Introspective paragraphs alternate with external events or dialogue to prevent lulls.

## 4. Dialogue Mechanics

- **Tags:** Minimalist: said and asked (or says and asks). Yelled only when someone actually yells. No decorative speech tags.
- **Action beats:** Prefer beats before or after dialogue over adverbs, to ground the speakers physically.
- **Hesitation & interruption:** Em-dashes (—) for cut-offs, ellipses (...) for trailing thoughts, awkward pauses, emotional stalling.
- **Shouting:** A shouted word can go in full caps. Keep it rare, and never use caps for emphasis in narration.
- **Subtext & restraint:** People rarely say exactly what they mean. Dialogue relies on subtext, hesitation, interruption, shared history, and what's left unsaid.
- **Written messages:** Letters, notes, signs, and messages go in italics.

## 5. Internal Monologue & Thought Formatting

- **Direct thoughts:** Clean italics without tags.
- **Nature:** Direct thoughts are punchy and reactive, in the POV's own Voice.

## 6. Action, Physicality, and Kinetic Energy

- **Emotion through action:** Show what the POV feels mostly through the action the user gave them and how they carry it out: specific to the moment and what's in their hands.
- **Body reactions:** Heat rising, a stomach drop, nausea, cold skin, a dry throat are a range to draw from, not a formula. Use them rarely, and never repeat one the log has used recently (Craft Rules, "No recycled body reactions").
- **Sensory zoom:** In heightened moments, perception sharpens down to small details: a single sound, one thing in their line of sight.
- **Violence:** Told at the level the Style Guide's content line allows, as cause and effect. What a blow did, not how impressive it was.

## 7. Tone & Vocabulary

- **Register:** Set per narrative in its Style Guide.
- **Descriptive lexicon:** Plain, concrete words this POV would use (Craft Rules, "Everyday words, not trade terms"). Strong simple descriptors over ornate, flowery, or abstract prose, unless the Style Guide gives the narration a different voice.

## Things to avoid while writing

- Over-description.
- Over-explaining.
- "The way" to describe something that doesn't need a description.
- Explaining or narrating backstory or character history.
- Banter written for the audience. All dialogue is rooted in the characters speaking and what they would actually say in that moment.
- The POV describing anything they couldn't see, hear, or smell.
- Anything that sounds like a game being narrated.

## Number 1 rule

TRUST the reader to understand what is on the page. DO NOT be afraid they will not understand what is written. DO NOT cater to readers who can't comprehend.
